Part 1 — Single-Scale Alignment

Brute-force NCC alignment on the small JPEG plates

The task

Each Prokudin-Gorskii plate is a single grayscale image stacked as three vertically-tiled exposures with blue on top, green in the middle, red on the bottom. I split the image into thirds by height, then align the green and red channels to the blue channel. My process is explained the sections below.


Throughout this writeup, dx and dy refer to that displacement: dx is the horizontal shift (columns) and dy is the vertical shift (rows) applied to a channel to bring it into alignment with blue.

Similarity metric

I used normalized cross-correlation (NCC). For two image patches a and b, I mean-center each (subtract its own average brightness), then compute:

\[ \text{NCC}(a, b) = \frac{a \cdot b}{\|a\|\,\|b\|} \]

This is the dot product of the two mean-centered images, normalized by the product of their L2 norms. It's invariant to overall brightness differences between channels. A score of 1 means a perfect match and -1 means perfectly anti-correlated. Higher NCC always means a better-aligned channel.

Search window

window = 15 px border crop = 10%

I used a search window of 15 pixels in each direction (so a 31×31 grid of candidate shifts), with a 10% border crop applied before scoring so the black plate edges don't corrupt the NCC signal. NCC is computed only on the interior region where two channels should actually agree. The full, untrimmed channel is still what gets shifted and saved.

I picked 15 by sweeping window sizes {5, 10, 15, 20} on cathedral.jpg:

  • At window=5 and window=10, the red channel's shift kept hitting the edge of the search window, which is a sign the window was too small to contain the true offset.
  • At window=15, both G and R settled on a stable answer — (2, 5) and (3, 12) respectively which did not change when I went up to window=20. This meant that window=15 is suitable.
Parameter sweep on border and window values

Algorithm walkthrough

For each candidate shift (dx, dy) in the search window:

  1. Shift the moving channel by (dx, dy) using np.roll.
  2. Crop both the shifted channel and the reference (blue) channel to the same interior region (trimming the border).
  3. Score the cropped pair with NCC.
  4. Keep the (dx, dy) with the highest score.

Once the best shift is found, I apply it to the full, uncropped channel, then stack the three channels into an RGB image and clip to [0, 1].

Post-processing: display crop

Shifting channels by (dx, dy) wraps pixels around the edges (a side effect of using np.roll), so the aligned output has a thin strip of misaligned pixels around its border. Before saving the final image, I apply a small fixed crop 7% on each side purely for display.

Before vs. after the fixed display crop

Discussion

All three test images aligned cleanly with a single 31×31 brute-force search with all comfortably inside the 15-pixel window. Runtime was near-instant for all three since the JPEGs are small.

Part 2 — Multi-Scale Pyramid Alignment

Extending single-scale alignment to full-resolution TIFFs via an image pyramid

Why single-scale brute force isn't enough

The full-resolution .tif plates are much larger than the JPEGs in Part 1, and their true channel offsets are correspondingly larger — some ended up over 100 pixels in a single axis. Brute-force search cost grows with both the window size and the number of pixels scored, so a 15-pixel window that ran instantly on a small JPEG takes far too long on a multi-megapixel TIFF, and a window wide enough to actually reach a 100+ pixel true offset would be even slower still.

The core problem

Single-scale search can't cover the right range of offsets in reasonable time at full resolution. It's a speed/range trade-off that brute force alone can't win.

How the pyramid works

pyramid_align is recursive:

  1. If the current image is already small (min(h, w) < min_size, using min_size = 100), run the Part 1 brute-force align() directly.
  2. Otherwise, downsample both the moving channel and the reference by a factor of 2 (img[::2, ::2]) and recurse on the smaller pair first.
  3. Double the shift returned from that coarser level to bring it to the current level's pixel scale, apply it, then run a small brute-force refinement search to correct for rounding error from downsampling.
  4. Sum the coarse and refine shifts and repeat going back up the pyramid until full resolution.

Each level only has to search a small local window because the level below already got the shift roughly right.

Search window per level

coarse (base case) = 15 px refine (every other level) = 4 px

At the coarsest (base-case) level: the same 15-pixel brute-force window as Part 1 — but since this level is heavily downsampled, 15 pixels there corresponds to a much larger displacement once scaled back up to full resolution. At every level above the base case, a small 4-pixel refinement window, since the shift estimate coming from the level below has already been scaled up and should be close to correct.

Metric

Same NCC function from Part 1. Scoring is still done on a 10%-border-cropped region at whichever resolution the current level is working at, so plate edge artifacts don't affect the metric.

Discussion

Across the 13 successfully-aligned images, runtime is consistent at roughly 14–16 seconds per full-resolution TIFF (under the 1 minute/image target), with the two small JPEGs finishing in under a third of a second. Offset magnitudes vary a lot by image as some (church, tobolsk) need only a few pixels of correction, while others (self_portrait, melons) need 100+ pixels. For every image except emir.tif, the G and R shifts move in a consistent direction relative to each other, which is a sign that the pyramid converged correctly.

Failure case — Emir of Bukhara

Failed alignment
G: (24, 49) R: (-778, 104)

Why plain pyramid alignment fails

Raw-pixel NCC gives an R shift wildly inconsistent with G, and completely out of line with every other image in the set. This plate's three exposures likely differ substantially in brightness/contrast from one another. Raw-pixel NCC is sensitive to exactly that kind of global intensity difference, so at the coarsest pyramid level — where there's little detail to distinguish real structure from lighting differences — the search converges on the wrong local optimum. Because each level only refines the shift handed down from the level below with a small 4-pixel window, a wrong answer at the coarsest level can never be corrected later; the error is locked in from the start.

Discussion

I picked a mix of architectural shots and images with a lot of detail, since misalignment shows up fastest as visible edges or fringing along straight lines and textured regions. A slightly-off shift is much easier to spot on a building facade or a detailed scene than on something flat or low-contrast. The results were satisfactory.

Bells & Whistles

Extensions beyond the core alignment pipeline

Gradient/Edge-Based Alignment

Bells & Whistles
Raw-pixel alignment
G: (24, 49) R: (-778, 104)
Gradient-based alignment
G: (24, 49) R: (40, 107)

Motivation & approach

The plain pyramid alignment from Part 2 failed on emir.tif, producing an R shift of (-778, 104) that's wildly inconsistent with its own G shift of (24, 49). The likely cause is that this plate's three exposures differ substantially in brightness from one another, and raw-pixel NCC is sensitive to that kind of global intensity difference. At the coarsest pyramid level, where there's little detail to go on, the search locks onto lighting differences instead of actual structure, and that wrong answer can't be recovered by the small refinement windows at finer levels.

My fix was to align on gradient magnitude images instead of raw pixels. For each channel, I run a Sobel filter in both x and y (skimage.filters.sobel) and combine them as:

\( G = \sqrt{G_x^2 + G_y^2} \)

gradient magnitude — edge strength at each pixel

Edge maps emphasize where the image changes rather than its absolute brightness, so they're far less sensitive to exposure differences between channels. I run the pyramid_align function from Part 2 on these gradient images to find the shift, then apply that shift to the original color channels to build the final output. The gradient images are only used to compute the offset.

Results

Switching to gradient-based alignment gives R shift = (40, 107), now consistent in direction and magnitude with the G shift of (24, 49), and in line with the range of offsets seen across the rest of the dataset. The comparison above shows the difference directly: the raw-pixel result has severe red ghosting while the gradient-based result produces a properly aligned color image. Total runtime was 17.4s.
